Strategy, speed, and scaled intelligence

 

Towards an integrative CIO competency framework for the AI / agentic era

academic work + industry reports + standards/regulation + practitioner benchmarks.

 

I’m using this McKinsey report as the anchor reference: “The new CIO mandate: Strategy, speed, and scaled intelligence” (Feb 2026)

 

1) What the new McKinsey Exhibit 5 adds: competency requirements as “constraints to scale”

McKinsey’s Exhibit 5 is unusually useful for theory-building because it operationalizes what blocks scaling agentic AI inside firms:

  • Talent/capability gaps (31%)
  • Integration complexity with existing systems/tools (29%)
  • Security/reliability/hallucinations (26%)
  • Regulatory/privacy/compliance (24%)
  • Lack of modern data foundations (21%)
  • Lack of clear business use cases (18%)
  • Difficulty measuring ROI/value (17%)
  • Internal resistance/change management challenges (16%)

 

This pattern strongly suggests a framing where CIO competencies are not “generic leadership skills,” but a portfolio of scaling constraints that the CIO function must remove across people, architecture, governance, data, and adoption.

 

The CIO shifts as moving from IT efficiency to technology velocity and positioning technology leadership as co-architects of enterprise strategy, a motivation to treat “strategy co-creation” as a first-class competency domain.

 

2) A research-backed way to structure an integrative CIO competency framework

 

Theoretical spine

Dynamic managerial capabilities (DMC) + Role theory + Governance-as-capability

  • Role theory lens on CIO in digital transformation: shows CIO role expands and becomes multidimensional in DT contexts. (Springer Nature)
  • Upper echelons / strategic orientation lens: empirical evidence that CIOs and boards shape a firm’s AI orientation (strategic direction toward AI). (MisQ)
  • Dynamic managerial capability lens for AI-driven leadership: recent work explicitly argues for leadership capabilities tailored to AI-driven environments. (journals.sagepub.com)
  • Governance capability lens: responsible AI governance is increasingly treated as a structured set of practices (structural, relational, procedural) with antecedents/effects. (sciencedirect.com)

This combination gives (a) legitimacy in IS scholarship, (b) a way to argue that competencies are microfoundations enabling organizational AI capabilities, and (c) a bridge to the practitioner constraints.

 

3) An integrative competency model

 

Domain A — Strategy Co-Creation and AI Value Architecture

Why: McKinsey shows top performers embed tech leadership in strategy and cocreate strategy iteratively.

Competency content (examples):

  • Translating enterprise strategy into an “agentic AI portfolio” (use cases + platform bets)
  • Continuous business–tech strategy co-creation (quarterly/continuous planning discipline)
  • Business-model innovation enabled by AI (data monetization, new digital revenue)

To cite:

  • Gartner’s CIO Agenda emphasizes capabilig AI-driven change and risk (useful benchmark language for “agenda-level” competencies). (gartner.com)
  • HBR’s GenAI playbook perspective gives leadership questions and competitive advantage framing. (Harvard Business Review)

Constraints addressed: lack of use cases; ROI/value measurement (partially); integration prioritization.

 

Domain B — Agentic Operating Model and Delivery Velocity (product/platform + engineering excellence)

Why: McKinsey links better performance to product/platform models, continuous decision-making, and engineering excellence.

Competency content:

  • Product/platform operating model design for AI-era delivery
  • Orchestration of cross-functional product teams (business+tech “two-in-the-box”)
  • Platform thinking (shared AI services, reusable components, standardized guardrails)

To cite:

  • Deloitte Tech Trends 2026 explicitly stresses that value comes from redesigning operations and building agent-compatible architectures + orchestration frameworks. (Deloitte)

Constraints addressed: integration of use cases; change management (via operating model).

 

Domain C — Enterprise Architecture and Integration (legacy → composable/AI-ready)

Why: integration complexity is #2 in McKinsey`s.

Competency content:

  • Architecting how agents safely “touch” enterprise systems (APIs, identity, approvals)
  • Legacy modernization pathways (incremental strangler patterns, integration middleware strategy)
  • Standardization of toolchains for LLM apps/agents (observability, eval, release pipelines)

To cite:

  • Enterprise architecture is explicitly called out as facing major AI-era integration demands. (Bizzdesign)
  • Practitioner guidance on LLMOps as operational management for LLM-based applications (a useful anchor for “delivery/integration competence”). (Microsoft Learn)

Constraints addressed: integraability; scaling.

 

Domain D — Data Foundations and “Data as a Product” (scaled intelligence precondition)

Why: lack of modern data foundations is a top barrier; McKinsey also highlights “data productization.”

Competency content:

  • Data governance, lineage, access control for GenAI/agentic workflows
  • Data product operating model (owners, SLAs, discoverability, semantics)
  • Knowledge architecture for enterprise retrieval (quality-controlled knowledge bases, RAG governance)

To cite:

  • IBM emphasizes data governance as the foundation for enterprise AI governance and risk mitigation. (IBM)
  • AWS highlights GenAI increases the number of data interactions, making governance/access control part of the workflow. (Amazon Web Services, Inc.)
  • MIT CISR voice on treating data like a product (excellent bridge between IS research and practice). (CIO)

Constraints addressed: lack of modern data foundations; hallucinations/reliability (via controlled knowledge); privacy.

 

Domain E — Trust, Risk, and Compliance Governance : security/reliability/hallucinations and regulatory/compliance together represent ~half the top constraints.

Competency content:

  • AI risk management as an enterprise system (not a checklist)
  • Responsible AI governance (structure, process, accountability)
  • AI security threat modeling for agentic workflows (prompt injection, data exfiltration, tool abuse)
  • Compliance translation (EU AI Act obligations; privacy guidance) into internal controls
    High-authority seeds (standards/regulation):
  • NIST AI RMF frames risk management into Govern, Map, Measure, Manage functions. (nvlpubs.nist.gov)
  • NIST GenAI Profile extends AI RMF specifically for generative AI risks/actions. (NIST)
  • ISO/IEC 42001 defines requirements for an AI management system (AIMS). (ISO)
  • ISO/IEC 23894 provides AI risk management guidance. (ISO)
  • COBIT for AI system governance: governance and management objectives adapted for AI contexts. (ISACA)
  • EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) official text and compliance framing. (EUR-Lex)
  • EP research note on implementation timeline helps contextualize “compliance capability maturity” as time-phased. (europarl.europa.eu)ance on GenAI and data protection provides a privacy-forward operational reference. (European Data Protection Supervisor)

Security-specific seeds:

  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (security awareness baseline). (owasp.org)
  • MITRE ATLAS threat knowledge base (adversary tactics/techniques vs AI systems). (atlas.mitre.org)
  • McKinsey’s playbook for deploying agentic AI with safety/security (CIO-relevant framing). (McKinsey & Company)

Constraints addressed: security/reliability/hallucinations; regulatory/privacy/compliance.

 

Domain F — Talent Systems and Capability Building (insource + reskill + targeted hire)

Why: talent/capability gaps are the #1 constraint.

Competency content:

  • Designing capability-led talent models (new roles, career paths, communities of practice)
  • Reskilling at scale + “AI literacy” governance
  • Vendor strategy that builds capability (not just capacity)

To cite:

  • McKinsey explicitly discusses insourcing + reskilling + targeted hiring to build genAI capability.
  • Bain press release: many executives cite lack of in-house expertise as slowing AI adoption; shows persistence of the talent gap. (Bain)
  • WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 offers macro evidence on skills disruption and workforce transformation strategies. (World Economic Forum)

Constraints addressed: talent/capability gaps; integration (through better teams); change management.

 

Domain G — Change Leadership and Human–AI Work Redesign (“superagency” → adoption at scale)

Why: internal resistance/change management is explicitly in Exhibit 5, and often underweighted in technical AI programs.

Competency content:

  • Designing human–AI workflows (not “bolt-on AI”)
  • Adoption instrumentation (usage, trust, exception handling, escalation paths)
  • Cultural enablement: psychological safety + learning + accountability

To cite:

  • McKinsey change management guidance: emphasizes outcome-based “North Star” and workflow reimagination for genAI adoption. (McKinsey & Company)
  • Empirical/academic: AI adoption and change management chapter emphasizes change management as key for successful AI adoption. (Springer Nature)
  • McKinsey “superagency” report frames leadership as a bottleneck to scale (useful for hypotheses about leadership capability). (McKinsey & Company)

Constraints addressed: resistance/change management; talent adoption; reliability (humans-in-the-loop).

 

4) integrative framework (conceptual model skeleton)

 

A. CIO Competency Portfolio (Domains A–G)
→ enables B. Enterprise Agentic AI Capabilities (organizational-level)
→ drives C. Outcomes (scale, velocity, trust, ROI)

Suggested “Enterprise Agentic AI Capability” mediators (measurable)

You can define 4–6 mediating capabilities, competencies and outcomes:

  1. AI Orientation / AI Strategic Intent (upper echelons link) (MisQ)
  2. Agentic Governance Maturity (NIST/ISO/COBIT crosswalk) (nvlpubs.nist.gov)
  3. AI-Ready Architecture & Integration Maturity (legacy + LLMOps readiness) (Microsoft Learn)
  4. Data Produity (data-as-product operating model) (CIO)
  5. Talent Capability Density (skills distribution, team composition, insourcing ratio) (Bain)
  6. Adoption & Work Redesign Readiness (workflow redesign, change mechanisms) (McKinsey & Company)

Outcomes:

  • Speed/velocity of deployment (cycle time, reuse, rollout pace)
  • Scale of agentic AI in production (coverage across workflows)
  • Trust outcomes (incident rates, policy compliance, auditability)
  • Business value (ROI proxies, productivity, revenue/cost outcomes)

 

5) Claims and “paper seeds”

 

Seed 1 — “CIO Agentic Governance Capability” (crosswalking standards into CIO competence)

Core claim: The CIO’s governance competence is now a composite capability 2001, and IT governance (COBIT), adapted to agentic threats (OWASP/MITRE).
Key sources: NIST AI RMF (nvlpubs.nist.gov); ISO 42001 (ISO); COBIT AI governance (ISACA); OWASP LLM Top 10 (owasp.org); MITRE ATLAS (atlas.mitre.org); EU AI Act (EUR-Lex).
Research questions:

  • How do CIO governance competencies translate external AI governance requirements into internal controls at scale?
  • What governance practices reduce “security/reliability/hallucination” barriers in Exhibit 5?

 

Seed 2 — “Integration is the hidden bottleneck: CIO architecture competence for agentic systems”

Core claim: Integration complexity is not just technical; it’s a socio-technical orchestration capability (architecture + identity + approval design + platform model).
Key sources: McKinsey Exhibit 5 ; Deloitte agentic AI strategy/orchestration (Deloitte); Microsoft LLMOps (Microsoft Learn).
Possible propositions:

  • Higher CIO competence in platform/product operating models is associated with lower perceived integration complexity and faster scaling.

 

Seed 3 — “Capability-led talent models: from outsourcing capacity to insourcing capability”

Core claim: The CIO competency shift includes talent system design (insource + reskill + hire) and is a leading indicator of agentic AI scaling capacity.
Key sources: McKinsey talent strategy (insourcing/reskilling/hiring) ; Bain talent gap (Bain); WEF Future of Jobs 2025 (World Economic Forum).
Research questions:

  • What talent governance practices (role definitions, communities, learning systems) predict higher agentic AI maturity?

 

Seed 4 — “Change leadership as the scaling multiplier: from pilots to workflow redesign”

Core claim: The hardest barrier is not tooling but workflow redesign + adoption governance; leadership behavior is the limiting factor (ties directly to “superagency” concept).
Key sources: McKinsey change management in genAI age (McKinsey & Company); McKinsey superagency (McKinsey & Company); Springer chapter on AI adoption & change management (Springer Nature).
Possible propositions:

  • CIO change-leadership competence moderates the relationship between AI platform maturity and realized business value.

7) A sprint plan

Work product 1: Competency × Constraints

  • Rows: McKinsey 8 constraints (Exhibit 5)
  • Columns: Competency domains A–G (above)
  • Cells: mechanism statements (“This competency reduces this constraint by…”)

Work product 2: Construct dictionary (2–4 pages)

For each competency domain:

  • Definition
  • Observable behaviors
  • Exroutines
  • Candidate metrics / survey items (even if rough)

Work product 3: Conceptual model + propositions (2 pages)

  • CIO competency portfolio → mediating enterprise capabilities → outcomes
  • 6–10 testable propositions (or research questions)

 

 

Job postings & skills intelligence (labor-market demand as competency signal)

What you get: market demand for specific skills/roles (e.g., “AI governance lead,” “LLMOps,” “agentic workflow designer,” “AI product manager”). Great for grounding “talent/capability gap” with observable signals.

  • EU JRC overview: online job ads used for skills intelligence and emerging digital skills
  • Example peer-reviewed method: mining thousands of job ads with topic modeling/content analysis to extract skill sets
  • O*NET database (tasks/skills statements; useful baseline for CIO occupation mapping)

How to use: build an “AI-era CIO competency delta” by comparing historical CIO skill baselines (O*NET) with emergent AI/agentic leadership skill signals from postings

Peer-reviewed syntheses you can “stand on” for construct definitions

What you get: academically defensible definitions and prior construct families (digital competencies, digital transformation capabilities) to avoid inventing everything from scratch.

  • Review/research agenda on digital transformation (large academic corpus synthesis)
  • Review of digital competencies (definitions and frameworks applicable at work)

How to use: anchor your competency domains in established construct families, then argue what changes in the agentic era (new sub-dimensions, boundary spanning, governance intensity, integration complexity).

Professional playbooks & vendor “operational guidance” (how work is actually done)

What you get: operational practices (LLMOps, evaluation, deployment patterns, platform components) that make integration/scale tangible.

  • (Example) Microsoft compliance/ISO 42001 context shows how large providers operationalize AI governance structures

How to use: treat these as “practice blueprints” to flesh out observable behaviors and routines behind each competency.

A practical way to use these sources (fast, publishable, no primary data)

A strong secondary-data method for AMCIS could be:

  1. Scoping review (peer-reviewed + standards/regulation) → defines competency domains and constructs.
  2. Structured content analysis of: (a) CIO/AI governance standards, (b) regulatory texts/guidance, (c) corporate disclosures, (d) job postings → derives sub-competencies and indicators.
  3. Triangulation matrix: each competency must be supported by at least two source types (e.g., survey + standards; disclosures + job ads).

This gives you methodological rigor without running interviews/surveys.

Secondary data sources that fit your angle

1) Trust & governance “hard anchors” (standards + playbooks)

Use these to define what “good” looks like and derive governance competencies as actionable routines.

  • NIST AI RMF 1.0 (Govern/Map/Measure/Manage) + AI RMF Playbook (concrete suggested actions)
  • NIST Generative AI Profile (GenAI-specific risk actions as companion to AI RMF)
  • ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management system requirements—great for “assurance-by-design” competency subdimensions)
  • ISACA COBIT guidance for AI system governance (connects AI governance to established IT governance objectives and controls)

How it supports the framework: lets you derive governance/trust competencies that are not vague (“be ethical”), but operational (risk mapping, lifecycle controls, accountability structures, measurement practices).

 

2) Regulation as “external constraint” (defines mandatory competency outcomes)

Use regulation to motivate why CIO trust/governance competence becomes strategic (license to operate + board-level risk).

  • EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) official text (risk-based obligations; governance, documentation, oversight implications)
  • (Optional strategic twist) SEC investor advisory materials on AI disclosure—useful for “AI risk becomes material disclosure” framing in some sectors

How it supports the framework: provides boundary conditions and makes governance competence measurable as “ability to translate obligations into architecture + controls + evidence.”

 

3) Security & agentic risk taxonomies (what can go wrong, specifically)

These help you operationalize “trust” beyond compliance—into technical and socio-technical controls.

  • OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (including a 2025 PDF version for concrete categories)
  • MITRE ATLAS threat matrix (adversary tactics & techniques against AI-enabled systems; great for agentic attack surfaces)

How it supports the framework: turns “security/reliability/hallucinations” into competency elements like LLM/agent threat modeling literacy, eval/monitoring governance, tool-use access control, incident response adaptation.

 

4) Real-world incident repositories (empirical “trust failures” you can code)

These give you observed outcomes and failure modes—excellent for validating whether your trust competencies cover reality.

  • Partnership on AI’s AI Incident Database (AIID) (1,200+ incidents; taxonomy-friendly)
  • OECD AI Incidents & Hazards Monitor (policy-facing incident framing; useful triangulation)
  • Stanford AI Index chapters on responsible AI and governance trends (good for macro context and trends)

How it supports the framework: you can code incident types (security, privacy, reliability, autonomy errors, etc.) and map them to missing governance/architecture safeguards—supporting your “competencies remove constraints” logic.

 

5) Integration/architecture “reference evidence” (how to build agentic systems in enterprises)

These sources are perfect for your integration/architecture domain because they spell out design patterns and operational considerations.

  • Google Cloud agentic AI design patterns (single-agent, multi-agent, orchestration patterns)
  • Microsoft Azure Well-Architected Framework for AI workloads (explicitly addresses nondeterminism, data/app design, operations, GenAIOps)
  • (Optional EA positioning) TOGAF (Enterprise Architecture) can serve as your “EA governance container,” even if you cite official access/download page rather than the full standard

How it supports the framework: provides concrete architectural competency primitives: orchestration, integration boundaries, identity & access, evaluation/monitoring, release governance, human-in-the-loop points, tool permissions, etc.

 

6) The “strategic twist”: CIO influence on AI orientation (peer-reviewed anchor)

You’ll want at least one top-tier IS anchor that directly legitimizes “CIO as shaper of AI strategy.”

  • MISQ paper on AI orientation: shows CIOs and boards affect development of firm-level AI orientation (upper echelons lens).

How it supports the framework: you can position governance + architecture not as “IT hygiene,” but as strategic capability enablers that let the firm pursue more ambitious AI orientation safely.

 

Method: “Triangulated Framework Synthesis” (scoping review + structured content analysis + crosswalk)

This is fast, defensible, and produces tangible artifacts (taxonomy + propositions).

Step 1 — Scoping review (2–3 weeks):

  • Peer-reviewed: CIO/AI orientation + digital leadership/governance literature (small, targeted set).
  • Practitioner/standards/regulation: NIST/ISO/COBIT + EU AI Act.

Step 2 — Crosswalk coding (2–3 weeks):
Build a crosswalk table:

  • Rows = governance requirements/outcomes (NIST AI RMF + GenAI profile + ISO 42001 + EU AI Act themes)
  • Columns = architectural controls/patterns (Google agentic patterns + Azure AI workload design + GenAIOps)
  • Output = competency statements as “capabilities to design and operate X controls/patterns to satisfy Y governance outcomes.”

Step 3 — Empirical grounding via incidents (1–2 weeks):

  • Sample (e.g., top 100–200 incidents from AIID plus OECD monitor highlights)
  • Code: failure mode → missing control → implied competency gap
  • Use this to validate coverage and to create “negative case” examples for the paper.

Step 4 — Strategic linkage (1 week):

  • Use the MISQ AI-orientation lens to propose how CIO competencies influence AI orientation and how governance/architecture maturity moderates the strategy→value relationship

 

with this design

  • A competency framework grounded in (a) authoritative governance standards, (b) real-world failure evidence, and (c) reference architectures for agentic systems.
  • A strategy linkage grounded in IS theory (AI orientation) rather than purely practitioner logic.
  • A set of testable propositions (even if you don’t test them now): e.g., governance capability × architecture maturity → safer scaling; AI orientation moderated by trust/architecture readiness.
