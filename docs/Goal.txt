secondary-data blueprint for “Strategic AI Orientation Enabled by Trust & Integration Readiness” that’s publishable at AMCIS without primary data, while still looking rigorous and “IS-theory-clean.”

1) Core idea and positioning

Thesis: CIOs shape a firm’s AI orientation (strategic intent/direction for AI), but the ability to realize that orientation depends on two enabling capability bundles: (1) trust/governance readiness and (2) integration/architecture readiness.

You can anchor the AI orientation construct in MISQ (upper echelons theory; CIO and board effects on AI orientation).

2) Best secondary data sources for your angle
A) Governance/trust readiness sources (normative + operationalizable)

Use these to define “trust readiness” as capabilities and controls, not vague ethics.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 + the Generative AI Profile (actions for GenAI risks; designed as companion to AI RMF).

EU AI Act official text (external compliance constraint; motivates board/CIO attention and makes governance strategic).

OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications + detailed vulnerability docs (e.g., prompt injection) to operationalize “trust threats” in agentic architectures.

MITRE ATLAS to ground “agentic threat surface” in a recognized adversarial taxonomy. (If you want, I’ll pull a direct MITRE ATLAS page next; we already have OWASP strongly sourced.)

What you extract: required practices, controls, governance structures, lifecycle checkpoints, assurance artifacts → become sub-competencies.

B) Integration/architecture readiness sources (design patterns + operating concerns)

Use these to specify what “integration readiness” means for agentic AI (orchestration, tool-use boundaries, nondeterminism, ops, evaluation).

Google Cloud: agentic AI system design patterns (single-agent vs multi-agent; requirements-driven selection).

Microsoft Azure Well-Architected: AI workload architecture guidance (explicitly treats nondeterminism, data/app design, operations) + GenAIOps/MLOps guidance.

What you extract: architectural primitives (orchestration patterns, integration boundaries, operational controls, evaluation/monitoring) → become integration/architecture sub-competencies.

C) “Reality checks” for trust failures (empirical grounding)

Use incident repositories to validate your trust readiness dimensions and create “negative cases.”

AI Incident Database (AIID) (large repository of real-world incidents)

OECD AI Incidents & Hazards Monitor (AIM) (policy-facing incident monitoring; trend framing)

What you extract: incident categories, root causes, governance/control breakdowns → map to missing competencies (“what went wrong” evidence).

D) Optional (but powerful) strategic twist source: public corporate discourse

If you have bandwidth, add 10-K/annual report risk sections + earnings call transcripts for a sample of firms to proxy “AI orientation” and “trust/integration emphasis” via text patterns.
(You can cite the MISQ AI-orientation paper for construct legitimacy and then explain your operationalization.)

3) A clean method design (secondary only) that looks rigorous
Step 1 — Construct definition & boundary (short scoping review)

Define AI orientation using MISQ.

Define trust readiness using NIST AI RMF + GenAI profile + EU AI Act + OWASP.

Define integration readiness using Google agentic patterns + Azure Well-Architected AI + GenAIOps.

Step 2 — Crosswalk content analysis (your “engine”)

Build a matrix:

Rows: governance outcomes/actions (NIST Govern/Map/Measure/Manage + GenAI profile actions; EU AI Act obligations)

Columns: architecture/integration mechanisms (agent patterns, nondeterminism controls, evaluation/monitoring, ops)

Output: a set of competency statements like:

“Translate risk obligations into tool-access controls and evaluation gates for agentic workflows.”

Step 3 — Incident validation (AIID + OECD AIM)

Code incidents into:

harm type (privacy, security, reliability, autonomy/tool misuse, etc.)

missing control (e.g., prompt injection defenses, access boundaries, monitoring)

implied competency gap (e.g., “agentic security threat modeling”, “eval governance”, “data boundary design”)

This strengthens your claim that your framework covers real failure modes.

4) Your conceptual model (ready for an AMCIS figure)

Enablers (your novelty)

Trust Readiness (governance capability bundle)

Integration Readiness (architecture capability bundle)

Mechanism

Trust & integration readiness mediate (or moderate) the AI orientation → realized value relationship by enabling safe scaling.

5) Starter propositions (you can include even without testing)

CIO presence/centrality → AI orientation (grounded in MISQ).

Higher trust readiness strengthens the conversion of AI orientation into production scaling (via reduced risk, fewer incidents, higher auditability), aligned to NIST AI RMF/GenAI actions.

Higher integration readiness strengthens the conversion of AI orientation into scaling (via reusable patterns, orchestration fit, and GenAIOps practices).

Trust readiness × integration readiness jointly matter (complements): weak architecture undermines governance; weak governance constrains architecture deployment. (Supported by how architecture guidance treats nondeterminism/ops and how OWASP shows agentic vulnerabilities.)

External regulatory pressure (e.g., EU AI Act) increases the strategic salience of trust readiness, pushing governance into the “AI orientation” agenda (license-to-operate effect).
